FreshKeep: helping households use food before it spoils

The problem

Food rarely goes to waste because people don't care. It goes to waste because they forget what they have. The information is printed on every packet, but it isn't in front of anyone at the moment they decide what to cook or buy.

The goal

Make checking the pantry and acting on it take seconds: one place that answers "what needs my attention right now?" and turns that answer into an action.

  1. 1
    Four tasks, four tabs

    Pantry, Alerts, Recipes and Stats map to the four things people come to do. The most frequent one, keeping the pantry, is the home tab.

  2. 2
    Adding comes first

    The add form sits at the top in one row: name, category, expiry, quantity. Expiry defaults to a few days ahead so most items take one change.

  3. 3
    Status said three ways

    Fresh, Expiring soon and Expired show as colour, icon and a word, with filters that match. Nobody has to rely on colour alone.

  4. 4
    Every card ends in an action

    Used and Remove sit on every card, and Donate appears on items about to expire, so noticing and acting happen in the same place.

  5. 5
    Help without a blank page

    The AI food assistant opens with suggestion chips like "Which items expire soon?", so starting a question takes one tap.

  6. 6
    Search for bigger pantries

    Once the list grows, search and filters keep any item two actions away.

What I'd change, looking at it now

  • Sort cards by urgency, so expiring items lead and long-expired ones collapse into one group instead of filling the screen.
  • Make the "11 expired" counter in the header a shortcut to the Expired filter.
  • Replace the small all-caps labels with sentence case for easier reading.

What I'd test next

  • Whether people find expiring items faster by colour or by "days left" sorting, measured as time to first correct tap.
  • Where eyes land first on a card, to check status really is read before the name.
  • Whether alerts feel helpful or nagging after a week, through short diary interviews.

BudgetBuddy: turning "what can we do for ₹500?" into five easy choices

The problem

Planning an outing with friends on a budget is an open, fuzzy decision. There are too many places, nobody knows what things cost, and the group ends up scrolling instead of going out.

The goal

Break the decision into a few small, easy choices, show the money trade-offs as you make them, and end with a plan the group can share.

  1. BudgetDrag to set a total
  2. VibeChill, slow or adventurous
  3. PeoplePer-person budget updates live
  4. CityEight Indian cities
  5. DistanceNearby or further out

  1. 1
    Errors that tell you what to do

    If location is blocked, the bar says so in plain words and offers one way forward: Detect Me. The plan still works by picking a city.

  2. 2
    Money as a gesture

    A slider with marked stops (₹100 to ₹5,000) is quicker than typing a number and shows the range at a glance.

  3. 3
    Mood before venues

    Choosing a vibe is easier than browsing a list of places, so the app asks how you feel first and picks places to match.

  4. 4
    Trade-offs shown live

    Budget per person updates the moment someone taps +, so the group sees what adding a friend means before committing.

  5. 5
    Every choice explained

    The distance options say what you get (easy to reach vs. hidden gems), and a line underneath confirms what the results will show.

  6. 6
    One clear finish

    A single full-width button says exactly what happens next. The plan shows spent, saved and per-person cost, a map, and Copy plan for the group chat.

What I'd change, looking at it now

  • Warn when the per-person budget gets too low to plan anything (₹50 each, as in the screen above) instead of letting people continue.
  • Show the sign-in screen's value before asking for Google sign-in, or let people try one plan without signing in.
  • Swap the spaced capital step labels for sentence case, which reads faster.

What I'd test next

  • Whether five steps on one scrolling page feel lighter than a one-step-per-screen wizard, comparing completion time and perceived effort.
  • How groups really decide: does one person plan and share, or do they plan together on one phone?
